From Ohlson 29 to Great Dane 28

I am not moving from 29 feet to 28. I am changing priorities.

My previous yacht, Ohlson 29 Rassvet, is close in length but belongs to a different branch of Scandinavian design: lighter, wider, fin-keeled and more responsive to wind and helm.

Great Dane should feel slower to accelerate, more reluctant to turn, less satisfying in very light wind and less cooperative in reverse. Tacks reward preserved momentum. Harbour manoeuvres reward planning.

Offshore, those same traits change sign. Four tonnes carry momentum through short sea. A long keel resists yaw. Protected underwater gear reduces exposed appendages. A windvane and tiller pilot can work with a boat that naturally wants to continue on course.

Giving upGaining
Light-air responsivenessInertia in rough water
Quick tacksCourse stability
Easy reverse controlProtected underwater profile
More beam and interior volumeMore ballast and conservative proportions
Short-passage speedLower workload offshore

The point is not a bigger yacht, but a platform better aligned with longer solo passages and northern offshore routes.

The Great Dane story

Static history, kept here as a reference.

This part of the site is deliberately lower down. It explains why the boat exists, but unlike voyages and refit work it does not change every season.

01

Klaus Baess

A Danish Dragon sailor and 1948 Olympic bronze medallist who exported Folkboats, understood the international small-yacht market and drove the production, deck, cockpit, interior and commercial concept of the Great Dane 28.

02

Aage Utzon

Ship engineer and designer of Denmark’s celebrated spidsgatter yachts. According to his grandson Jan Utzon, Aage was responsible for the Great Dane’s hull, freeboard, keel and rudder: the part of the boat that meets the sea.

03

Folkboat + Sisu

The Nordic Folkboat contributed compact simplicity and fine lines; Utzon’s heavier Sisu spidsgatter contributed ballast, stability and security in rough weather. Great Dane kept the philosophy but adopted a transom stern.

1948Baess wins Olympic bronze in the Dragon class and builds North American contacts.
1950sBaess exports Folkboats and works with the heavier Utzon-designed Sisu.
c. 1960A wooden Great Dane prototype precedes the production design.
1965–66Series production begins in hand-laid GRP.
1967Great Dane 28 reaches the London Boat Show and UK market.
1969Baess wins the Yachting World One-of-a-Kind Rally, judging cruising suitability among 25–30 ft yachts.
1980After roughly 250 boats, production ends and the original moulds are destroyed.

Design philosophy

The compromises are the character.

01

Mass instead of nervousness

About four tonnes on 28 ft means slower acceleration in light air, but less abrupt response to short sea and more retained momentum.

02

Ballast instead of beam

At only about 2.5 m wide, Great Dane does not get its character from maximum beam. Roughly 1.8 tonnes of lead sit low in the long keel.

03

Course-keeping over agility

The long keel and protected rudder geometry favour directional stability. The cost is more turning radius and less enthusiasm in reverse.

04

Sea room over showroom

The narrow hull limits interior volume. That space was not forgotten; it simply was not the first design objective.

Size may limit speed and comfort. It does not necessarily have to limit the sea.

Scandinavian context

Familiar ideas, unusually concentrated.
