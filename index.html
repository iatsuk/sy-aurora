The lineage

Not a small boat made bigger. A sea boat made small.

Great Dane 28 appeared at a short historical moment when traditional Scandinavian ideas about seaworthiness met fibreglass production. Its proportions look conservative today because they were conservative on purpose.

01

Klaus Baess

A Danish Dragon sailor and 1948 Olympic bronze medallist who turned North American contacts into a boat-export business. He sold Folkboats abroad, understood what owners actually bought and used, and later drove the production, deck, cockpit, interior and commercial concept of the Great Dane 28.

02

Aage Utzon

Ship engineer and designer of Denmark’s celebrated spidsgatter yachts. According to his grandson Jan Utzon, Aage was responsible for the Great Dane’s hull, freeboard, keel and rudder: the part of the boat that meets the sea. He was also the father of Sydney Opera House architect Jørn Utzon.

03

Folkboat + Sisu

The design was influenced by two different ancestors. The Nordic Folkboat contributed compact simplicity and fine lines; Utzon’s heavier Sisu spidsgatter contributed a philosophy of ballast, stability and security in rough weather. Great Dane kept the spirit, but used a transom stern.

Design philosophy

The compromises are the character.

01

Mass instead of nervousness

About four tonnes on 28 ft is a lot. It means inertia: slower acceleration in light air, but less abrupt response to every short wave. The boat is intended to keep moving with composure rather than react instantly to everything around it.

02

Ballast instead of beam

At only about 2.5 m wide, the hull does not create stability primarily through width. Roughly 1.8 tonnes of ballast sit low in the long keel. The result is a narrow, heavy and deliberately conservative package rather than a wide floating apartment.

03

Course-keeping over agility

The long keel and transom-hung rudder favour directional stability and protected underwater gear. The price is predictable: more turning radius, less enthusiasm in reverse and a need to plan harbour manoeuvres before they become urgent.

04

Sea room over showroom

A 2.5 m beam limits interior volume. That space was not “forgotten”; it was never the main objective. Great Dane prioritises a narrow hull, deep structural keel, restrained sail plan and manageable behaviour offshore.

Size may limit speed and comfort. It does not necessarily have to limit the sea.

Where it sits among Scandinavian classics

Familiar ideas, unusually concentrated.

No single Swedish boat is “the same” as a Great Dane 28. Laurin 28 is probably the closest technical relative; Storfidra shares the pocket-offshore philosophy; Vindö 32 and Hallberg-Rassy Monsun show how unusual it is to put nearly four tonnes into only 28 feet.

From Ohlson 29 to Great Dane 28

I am not moving from 29 feet to 28. I am changing priorities.

My previous yacht, Ohlson 29 Rassvet, is close in length but belongs to a different branch of Scandinavian design: about 3.3 tonnes, 2.7 m wide, fin keel, more sail area relative to weight and a quicker response to wind and helm.

Great Dane should feel slower to accelerate, more reluctant to turn, less satisfying in very light wind and less cooperative in reverse. Tacks will reward preserved momentum instead of aggressive helm. Harbour manoeuvres will reward planning instead of correction.

But offshore those same traits change sign. Four tonnes carry momentum through short sea. A long keel resists yaw. A protected propeller aperture and keel-hung geometry reduce exposed underwater appendages. A windvane and tiller pilot can work with a boat that naturally wants to continue on course.

That is the point of the move: not a bigger yacht, but a platform better aligned with longer solo passages and northern offshore routes.
